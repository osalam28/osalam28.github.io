AI-Driven Personalization at Scale

How I built a recommendation engine that increased content engagement by 3× and contributed 11% lift in AUM — earning a U.S. patent in the process.

Timeline

14 months

Team Size

Cross-functional: PM, DS, Eng, Design

My Role

Product Lead

The Problem

A Library No One Could Navigate

The wealth management platform had built an extensive content library over the years: white papers on market trends, calculators for retirement planning, educational videos on portfolio diversification, and interactive tools for tax optimization.

The content was high-quality. The problem was that customers couldn't find what they needed when they needed it.

The platform relied on keyword search and manual categorization. If a customer searched for "retirement," they'd get 200 results sorted by recency. If they browsed by category, they'd see every piece of content tagged "retirement planning" with no indication of what was relevant to their situation.

Usage data told the story:

  • Only 12% of customers engaged with educational content in a given month
  • High-value tools like the retirement calculator had less than 5% adoption despite being featured prominently
  • Customer surveys showed people wanted more guidance, but they couldn't figure out where to start
"Customers weren't looking for more content. They were looking for the right content at the right time."
The Insight

Flip the Model

Most content platforms are built around search: the customer knows what they want and searches for it. But in wealth management, customers often don't know what questions to ask. They're not searching for "529 plan contribution limits" — they're trying to figure out how to save for their kid's college.

The breakthrough insight was simple: stop making customers search. Start surfacing the right content based on where they are in their financial journey.

This required shifting from a passive content library to an active recommendation engine. Instead of "here's everything we have," we needed to say "based on your situation, here's what you need next."

The Build

How We Built It

Building a recommendation engine sounds straightforward. In practice, it required solving three hard problems: defining relevance, building trust, and proving it worked before full rollout.

Step 1: Define What "Relevance" Actually Means

I partnered with data science to map the customer journey and identify the behavioral signals that indicated intent. We analyzed:

  • Account data: Age, account balance, investment mix, recent transactions
  • Behavioral signals: What pages they visited, what tools they used, how long they spent on content
  • Life events: Major deposits (new job, inheritance), withdrawals (home purchase), or account changes (retirement transition)

We built personas around these signals. A 28-year-old with a new $50K deposit who's been viewing mortgage calculators is probably buying a house. A 62-year-old who just moved assets from a 401(k) to an IRA is probably retiring soon. The content we surface for each persona should be different.

Step 2: Build Guardrails to Avoid Creepy Over-Personalization

AI can be powerful, but it can also be creepy. We didn't want customers to feel like we were watching them too closely or making assumptions about their personal lives.

We established product principles:

  • Transparency: Always show customers why we're recommending something ("Based on your recent activity...")
  • Control: Let customers dismiss recommendations or tell us they're not relevant
  • Privacy: Never use personally identifiable information in ways that would surprise customers

These guardrails weren't just ethical — they were product requirements. If customers didn't trust the recommendations, they wouldn't use them.

Step 3: Validate Before We Scaled

Before rolling out the recommendation engine to all customers, we ran a controlled experiment with 10% of users. Half saw personalized recommendations. Half saw the standard content library.

We measured:

  • Content engagement rate (did people click on recommendations?)
  • Tool adoption (did recommendations drive usage of high-value tools?)
  • Customer satisfaction (did people find the experience helpful or intrusive?)
  • Business impact (did engaged customers have higher AUM contribution?)

The results were clear: the recommendation group had 3× higher engagement with educational content and 11% higher AUM contribution over six months. Customer feedback was overwhelmingly positive.

Only after we had proof did we roll it out to the full customer base.

The Recommendation Engine Architecture

Input Layer: Behavioral signals, account data, content metadata

Processing Layer: Machine learning model trained on historical engagement data to predict relevance

Ranking Layer: Personalized content ranking based on predicted engagement likelihood

Output Layer: Contextual recommendations surfaced in-app and via email

But the numbers only tell part of the story. Here's what actually changed:

High-value tools finally got adopted

The retirement calculator — previously used by less than 5% of customers — saw adoption jump to 18% within six months. Why? Because we stopped expecting customers to search for it and started surfacing it to people approaching retirement.

Customers engaged longer with the platform

Average session duration increased by 40%. Customers who engaged with recommended content visited the platform 2.5× more frequently. This wasn't just vanity metrics — more engaged customers contributed more assets.

We earned a U.S. patent

The recommendation model we built was novel enough to earn a U.S. patent. This wasn't the goal — the goal was solving a customer problem — but it validated that we'd built something genuinely innovative.

The team learned how to ship AI responsibly

This project became the blueprint for how the company approached AI product work. Start with the problem, not the tech. Build guardrails early. Measure impact rigorously. Ship iteratively.

What I Learned

AI Products Succeed When They Solve Real Problems

I've seen companies build AI features because "everyone's doing AI" or because it sounds impressive in a pitch deck. Most of those features fail.

This project succeeded because we started with a customer problem — not a technology looking for a use case. The AI was a means to an end, not the end itself.

Personalization requires trust

The best recommendation engine in the world is useless if customers don't trust it. We spent as much time on transparency and control mechanisms as we did on the algorithm. That investment paid off in adoption.

Measure business outcomes, not just model accuracy

Data science teams love optimizing for precision and recall. But the real question isn't "is the model accurate?" — it's "does this change customer behavior in ways that matter to the business?"

We tracked model performance, but we held ourselves accountable to engagement rates and AUM contribution. That kept the team focused on outcomes, not just technical elegance.

Start small, prove value, then scale

We could have tried to roll out the recommendation engine to all customers on day one. Instead, we ran a controlled experiment with 10% of users. This gave us room to learn, iterate, and fix problems before they became catastrophic.

The upfront investment in rigor saved us months of rework and earned trust from leadership.

"AI is only valuable if it solves a real problem. The tech was the easy part. The hard part was defining success, earning customer trust, and making sure the team understood why we were building it."
