Building an Operating Model That Protects Focus

The Challenge

Teams were shipping features, but outcomes were inconsistent. Teams were proud of their velocity, but leadership couldn't connect the work to business outcomes. Accountability was fuzzy.

What I Did

I rebuilt how the team worked: shifted from feature roadmaps to outcome-based planning, introduced OKRs that protected focus, and built a discovery cadence that validated assumptions before writing code.

This wasn't about adding process — it was about creating clarity on what success looked like and giving teams the authority to kill low-impact work.

AI-Driven Personalization at Scale

The Challenge

A wealth management platform had built a massive content library — white papers, calculators, market insights — but customers weren't finding what they needed. Search was terrible. Engagement was low. High-value tools were getting ignored.

What I Did

I led the build of a recommendation engine that used behavioral signals to surface the right content at the right time. We flipped the experience from passive search to intent-based discovery.

This required partnering with data science to define "relevance," building guardrails to avoid creepy over-personalization, and running controlled experiments before rollout.

How I Think

Four principles that guide how I approach product leadership.

Measurement isn't optional

If you can't measure it, you can't improve it. I set up feedback loops early — qualitative and quantitative — so we know if we're winning or just busy. Strong measurement tells you what to do next and reduces rework.

AI should solve problems, not impress people

I've shipped AI products that users loved and AI products that were technically impressive but useless. The difference? Starting with the problem, not the tech. Most teams build AI features because it's exciting, not because it solves a real customer pain.

Teams need protection from chaos

Roadmaps should say "no" more than they say "yes." My job is to protect the team from distractions so they can focus on work that compounds. OKRs aren't bureaucracy — they're shields against feature creep.

Process should enable, not slow down

I've run agile transformations, scaled global teams, and introduced OKRs — but only when they solve a real coordination problem. Process for the sake of process is bureaucracy. The goal is clarity, not documentation.
